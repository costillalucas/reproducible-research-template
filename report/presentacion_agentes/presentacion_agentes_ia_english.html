A month with AI agents on a Fourier ptychography problem

What was solved and what was not

Lucas Costilla · week of 28 Sep 2026

Introduce yourself. Say in one sentence what this is about: for a month we asked artificial-intelligence agents to work on a real microscopy problem. The talk is not about the technique but about what they could and could not solve.

The problem in one minute

A microscope cannot separate details below a certain limit: with our objective, ~3.8 µm.

FPM takes NxN photos, each with the light coming from a different angle, and a program combines them to reach, in theory, 0.5–1 µm.

The hard part: the program has to know the exact geometry and infer something the camera does not record, how much the light is delayed (the phase).

2 / 14

Explain with the jigsaw analogy: each light angle brings a different piece of the fine detail. Stress that the program always returns an image; the problem is knowing whether that image is the sample or an artefact. That question runs through the whole talk. The numbers: 3.8 µm is the Abbe limit, lambda/(2·NA), with 530 nm green light and NA 0.07 (in red, 4.5 µm). With the LEDs that have useful signal, up to about 6 steps from the centre (sine of the angle ≈ 0.44), the theoretical limit drops to lambda/(2·(NA + 0.44)) ≈ 0.5 µm. In our setup that is 13×13 = 169 photos.

3 / 14

Give the conclusion up front so everyone knows where the talk is going. What was achieved is mostly infrastructure and auditing. What was not achieved is the final scientific goal: a real image with more detail that we trust. The red reconstruction from Friday the 25th is the first one that looks like the sample, but it still shows no new detail. The five errors: in our program, the frozen solver, the scale of the initial image and the uncorrected exposure; in the data, the reference image without detail and the wrong objective in the configuration. The three problems in Lucas's earlier code are separate. The centre of the matrix was measured from the images; the height z cannot be pinned down from the images, so the hand measurement (74 mm) was used. On resolution: the spheres are large and a single photo already separates them, but the two-halves test does not need small objects; in simulation with similar particles it passes and on real data it does not, so something in the model or the data is also failing. Important distinction: predicting photos the reconstruction did not use proves that it recovers the real phase of the sample (and the test can fail: it failed in the zone with volume scattering). It does not prove a resolution gain, because each unseen photo shares much of its spectrum with its neighbours; that is what the two-halves test measures, and it does not pass yet.

We make up a sample, simulate the 169 photos and the program reconstructs from them. Since we know the answer, we can measure how close it gets. This is the foundation: if it did not work here, there would be no point in trying real data. The numbers come from the simulation with the real geometry (matrix at 75 mm, 100 iterations, no noise). With the matrix at 100 mm, which gives more overlap between neighbouring LEDs, the phase rises from 0.87 to 0.97: an argument for changing the geometry in the next captures.

The worst error: on real data, the program did nothing

The program corrects the image a little in each round. Those corrections were ~8,000 times smaller than they should have been: it returned almost the original photo.

Hundreds of automated tests passed, because they used images of 16 to 32 pixels.

An agent investigating something else found it. Everything done before on real data had to be discarded.

Fixing the step was necessary but not enough: with the December data the result was noise. Credible reconstructions came with the September captures.

6 / 14

What the step is: the program improves the image gradually; it compares the photos it predicts with the measured ones and corrects it a little in each round. Analogy: walking down a mountain in steps of a tenth of a millimetre; after 100 steps you are still where you started. The program delivered the starting photo as if it were the reconstruction. Lesson: passing every test does not guarantee the code works in the real case. Where the 8,000 comes from: the program divided the correction by the number of pixels in the photo (160,000 at 400×400), which is correct, but it used a fixed step of 20 tuned on tests of 16 to 32 pixels, where 20 is reasonable. With real photos, 20/160,000 is 1/8,000 of the normal step. It was not intuitive because it depends on the image size.

7 / 14

Several errors had been introduced or missed by the agents themselves, and other agents found them while auditing with a different question. The reference one is telling: the image we validated against had less detail than a single raw photo, so the best 'reconstruction' was the unprocessed photo. It was taken with another microscope (Zeiss), but with a 2.5x/0.075 objective and 2.34 µm per pixel: a useful reference would be the 10x/0.25 on that same Zeiss. The objective: the program defaulted to the 2.5x/0.07 and the December capture was taken with the 2x/0.10; nobody asked until the person confirmed the hardware. The noise: in the dark frames, the verifier measured 2.6 counts of temporal noise; the assistant had explained the 5.4-count spread of the dark map as dark-current shot noise, when it actually came from hot pixels (a fixed pattern of the sensor).

10 / 14

New capture from Friday the 25th, red only, and now with no-sample photos and dark frames for correction. Same four criteria, fixed before looking. It predicts the photos it did not see with error 0.52, against 0.99 without reconstructing. Number 3: by eye the grid pattern almost disappears, but the number did not drop by half, which was the requirement. Number 4: the new particles repeat in both halves, but they are phase within what the objective already resolves. The criteria were calibrated beforehand with a simulation of this same capture. It is the first real reconstruction that looks like the sample: you see the particles and their phase, which no photo records. But the central test still fails: if we reconstruct separately with half of the LEDs and with the other half, they agree up to the objective's limit and beyond it they do not agree at all. In other words: we recover the phase, we do not gain resolution. Three things from that afternoon serve as lessons about agents: not even the simulation passed the thresholds written in advance, and they had to be corrected before touching the real data, with that written down; the agent team's own hypothesis (that the grid came from not weighting each photo by its light) was tested and turned out false, because weighting makes it worse; and the grid pattern came partly from how the reconstruction was started.

11 / 14

On Monday the 28th the lab took the same field in all three colours, with the focus fixed on green and no-sample photos. It is the first multispectral test with real data, which is what the project title promised. The raw photos of the three colours agree well with each other, between 0.71 and 0.78: it is the same field. But the reconstructions only agree in what the objective already sees, up to 0.52, and in the fine detail almost not at all, 0.06 at most. If that detail belonged to the sample, it would have to appear in all three colours. Also, only green, the colour in focus, looks like the sample; in red and blue the particles come out enlarged and with a grid texture, and refocusing them numerically does not fix it. In other words: the real multispectral combination cannot be done yet, because first each colour has to give a reliable reconstruction. 12 / 14

Last capture, Monday the 28th in the evening. We changed two things at once: the matrix went from 74 to 98 millimetres, which raises the overlap between neighbouring LEDs from 31 to 46 percent, and each colour was focused separately. The agents computed each LED's exposure time from the previous capture, and it was corrected on the fly with what was being measured. Result: all three colours predict the photos they did not see, with error between 0.46 and 0.62, against 0.98 without reconstructing; red gives the best value of the project. By eye, the three colours reconstruct the same particles in the same places; in the previous take red and blue were blobs. The two halves of LEDs agree in the fine detail between 5 and 14 times more than by chance, but still below the threshold we set. In other words: combining the colours now makes sense, but the resolution gain is not shown yet. And since we changed two things together, we do not know which helped more.

Lessons for any group using agents

1

Criteria before results

Write down what counts as success before running on real data

2

Independent verification

Another agent, with different code, redoes every number

3

Every number with its source

The file that produces it, cited and checkable

4

Simulation first

Where the answer is known, under the same conditions

5

A person who knows the equipment

The agent cannot answer the hardware questions

They contribute: volume, reviewing code and numbers for errors, experiment design. They fail at: the physical world and overconfidence.
