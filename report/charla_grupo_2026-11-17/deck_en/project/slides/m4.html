Mini-course · 2 · Illumination and Fourier

Our microscope seen in Fourier space

Our setup · real data from 13/11 (red, LED (17,16), 18 ms, minus dark) · Δx = 1.29 µm/px

(1.5 min) With real data. On the left, the photo with the LED next to the axis (bright field). In the centre, its Fourier transform: two equal circles are visible, shifted relative to each other. The radius of each circle is NA/λ; the shift is k_LED = sin θ/λ. Fitting the edge: radius 0.115 µm⁻¹ (166 Fourier pixels) → NA ≈ 0.072 with λ = 0.63 µm (0.071 with the measured λ = 0.617). Shift 0.098 µm⁻¹ → sin θ ≈ 0.062, close to the 0.060 given by the geometry (LED at 6 mm, z ≈ 99.5 mm). Why two circles: the camera measures |ψ|², and the transform of an intensity is the autocorrelation of the field spectrum. The dominant term is direct light × diffracted light: the pupil disc translated so that the zero order falls at the origin. Since the image is real, its transform has Hermitian symmetry, Ĩ(−k) = Ĩ(k)*, so that disc appears with a symmetric twin: two circles at ±k_LED. This happens in every bright-field photo of a weak sample: with the on-axis LED the two circles coincide; in dark field there is no zero order and they disappear (only a faint halo remains). With strong samples the diffracted × diffracted term smears the edges: that is why this method depends on the sample. These two numbers are what the algorithm needs to place each photo in the spectrum: if they are wrong, the pieces do not fit. Technical detail: the algorithm only sees quantities in Fourier pixels (the Fourier pixel is 1/(N·Δx)); the pupil depends on NA·Δx and the LEDs on Δx·(position)/z. If Δx is scaled by c, NA is divided by c and z is multiplied by c, the model does not change: that is why Δx is measured separately, with a micrometer. Fitting these circles is the basis of the self-calibration by Eckert et al. (2018). In October, this method gave r ≈ 170 px (NA ≈ 0.074); today the edge gives 0.070–0.072.
