Audience question · 3 of 3

Every reconstruction looks sharper than the photo. What experiment would convince you that the new detail is real and not an artefact of the algorithm?

FPM Mini-course · one click per test, from the most obvious to the least obvious · illustrative thumbnails (simulation)

(2 min) Gather ideas from the audience before revealing. One click per test, from the most obvious to the least obvious. 1) Known object: if bars of known size are resolved in the reconstruction but not in the photo, the detail is real. This is the best option, but there is no USAF target at LEC. 2) Predict the unseen: hold out some LEDs, reconstruct without them and check whether the model predicts those photos. This is our criterion C1; it measures whether the model is consistent with the data, not the resolution. 3) Two independent halves: split the LEDs into two groups (checkerboard), reconstruct each half separately and check whether they agree at the frequencies that the objective alone does not see (FRC). This is our criterion C2. 4) Null control: reconstruct with the same algorithm after deliberately shuffling the dark-field photos among LEDs (each photo is assigned to the wrong angle). This destroys exactly the information that would give super-resolution, but preserves everything else (amount of light, noise, the algorithm itself). If fine detail still appears, the algorithm invents it; if it disappears, it came from the dark field. Every metric (C1, C2, the sphere width) is compared against the same metric in the null. It returns in the results: the first C2 that passes (0.185 versus 0.008 in the null) and the 0.5 µm spheres (null 4.7–5.5 µm versus FPM 3.0–3.2 µm). The thumbnails are illustrative; they do not come from the solver or from real data.
